Keeping an edit and its translation in sync.

I worked with Codex on a recorded-event remaster, then used a timing map to fit an existing Spanish recording to the revised edit.

On this page The edit Three timelines The method The corrections What we checked Still open

It started with the graphics

I needed information cards for a long recorded event. I had the copy in a spreadsheet and an existing animation to work from. That grew into a remaster with new graphics, still images, removals, and audio repairs.

There was also a Spanish recording made against the earlier English version. Every change to the edit could affect where that translation belonged.

I had mostly deleted things and left gaps, but there were a few moments where I had to shift things, and some ended up extending a bit. Keeping the gaps was no longer enough to preserve the original timing.

Three timelines to keep track of

We needed to map where each retained passage came from and where it ended up. The version labeled "with gaps" already included my extensions, so its name couldn't tell us which clock we were looking at.

Some empty stretches were there because I wanted a pause. I wanted a little breather at the end, for example. Those needed to stay when we closed the gaps left by removed material.

A map between the versions

Codex turned the editing notes into a cue sheet with timings, assets, and review states. We used whole frames at the sequence's verified 25 fps. I reviewed the planned actions before each editing pass, and the saved project was checked afterward.

The initial pass placed 48 visual inserts: 19 information cards and 29 still images. Removals first left gaps. Once I had finished my refinements, 4 selected gaps were closed from the end backward, with the other sequences protected.

For the Spanish audio, the map selected 7 retained source segments and placed them in the final edit, with 6 intentional silent intervals. Each segment kept its playback speed. The original recording stayed on a muted track, and the conformed audio went on a separate track for review.

We also made a bilingual timing-review document to help locate passages. Its speech-recognition text could guide listening, but still needed checking against the audio.

What needed another pass

Moving the clips hadn't settled the transitions. Their positions were stored separately and needed a correction pass. The saved report records 133 transitions moved and 12 kept in place.

The graphics delivery also needed a correction in how it was described. We had rendered cards and structured copy data, but the promised editable After Effects builder was missing. That remained a limitation of this delivery.

An English transcript exported before the final timing changes was stale. I re-transcribed the finished English sequence so the review material would follow the edit we were actually using.

What the saved work supports

The result was a remastered installment and a Spanish audio version prepared for listening review. I made the editorial refinements and handled the exports. Codex supplied the placement scripts, timing map, and verification tools.

Preparing this case study included recounting the card files and data: 20 of each, including a later short card. Comparing saved snapshots from the gap-closing pass confirmed that 6 other sequences were unchanged.

The audio file matched the sample count planned by the map, and all 6 intended silence windows contained zero-valued samples. Its file hash also matched the saved verification record.

These checks combine saved production reports with later inspection of retained files. They don't amount to a fresh playback review of the whole project.

The listening review still matters

The saved checklist still had listening and human approval open. Matching the file's duration can't tell us whether a phrase ends naturally or whether the translation says what it should.

The translated-card workbook also had 20 rows pending review. It was preparation for a graphics batch, with no completed translated renders established in this record.

The work produced separate workflow specifications for remaster editing and audio conforming. Keeping the audio workflow focused means graphics and captions get their own review. If the finished edit changes again, the timing map needs to be rebuilt and checked against it.
