AI Portfolio

Built to stop and ask.

Fourteen AI tools I built for my own work over the past year. What they have in common is where they stop.

How these are built

Every one of them follows the same shape, and I didn't set out to follow it. I kept arriving at it, because I was building for someone whose work I couldn't afford to damage.

Most teams I talk to have the same worry about AI, and it isn't about capability. They're afraid of finding out afterward that something went out in their name that they never approved.

Not all of these were my idea. One came from pointing at a year of project history and asking what was worth building, which turned up a set of checks I had been doing by hand every time and never written down. Asking that question is a habit worth having.

It reads first and changes nothing.

It shows me what it found.

Then it waits.

Nothing gets built until I say so, and nothing gets written anywhere except one folder I empty myself.

What they refuse to do

Almost every one of these tools has a section in it titled "What this skill is not." These are lines from those sections, as written.

Findings are observations, not action items. A tool that walks through my notes reports what it sees and stops there. It doesn't hand me a task list I didn't ask for.

Never a guilt engine. One of these finds the parts of my archive I've stopped visiting. If a finding would land as "you neglected this," it gets reframed or dropped.

No hooks, no scheduled runs, no committing behind my back. The tool that version-controls my writing never runs on its own. Every save point is taken on purpose.

It never invents something the material didn't ask for. The tool that proposes new notes can only propose what the sources already pointed at twice.

It never creates missing files, edits links, renames anything, or updates the registry. It reads, it reports, and any repair is a separate job I have to authorize.

The raw text is preserved exactly. Nothing is rewritten, summarized, or cleaned up. The annotation tool adds structure around my words and never touches the words.

A few of them

Map-maker

You have a pile. Files, pages, transcripts, a folder nobody has opened in a year. Map-maker reads all of it and gives you one place to read it from, in whatever format you want: a web page, a Word doc, a PDF, a deck.

The part I care about is that it doesn't cut you off from your own material. Every statement on the map carries a coordinate back to where it came from, precise enough to act on: a filename and a heading, a page number, a timestamp. You read the consolidated view, hit something you need to be sure about, and go straight to the exact spot in the source.

Where a line pulls several sources together and has no single origin, it says so. You can always tell what came from your material and what was added to connect it.

Handoff

Long pieces of work outlive a single sitting. Handoff writes the note that lets the next session pick up without losing what this one learned: what was decided, what was ruled out and why, what is still open, and the one next thing to do.

It checks its own claims against the files before writing them down, because notes like this go stale quietly and nobody notices until they've acted on one. It also marks which lines are verified and which are just the last session's read, so an assumption can't get laundered into a fact three sessions later.

Ingest-transcript

Anyone publishing long recorded conversations has the same problem: someone has to get through a two hour transcript and decide what to cut before it ships. This reads the whole thing and hands back one timestamped report. What has to come out because a speaker asked for it on the record. What's worth listening to again before deciding. The standout lines worth pulling as clips. And any passage where someone recited from a published text long enough to earn a quote card on screen.

It asks one question before it starts: what comes before this transcript in the finished file, and how long does it run. Every time it reports is then offset to match the editor's timeline instead of the transcript's. Without that, someone does the arithmetic by hand for every entry, which is worse than getting no timestamps at all.

It began as a tool I built for a nonprofit I work with, narrowed to the one text they quote from constantly. Generalizing it was the obvious move once I saw it working, because a podcast host quoting four different books has the harder version of the same problem.

It doesn't decide anything. The editor does.

Save points

I'm not a professional developer, and version control is the single most useful thing I've learned that most people never get near, because the tooling assumes you already speak it.

This wraps it in plain language. It tells me what changed since last time in ordinary words, proposes a description, and commits when I say yes. Anything that could lose work gets explained in full before it runs, and it teaches me one small thing per session when there's a natural opening.

The rest of them

These tend my own writing archive, so the subject matter is mine. The design decisions are the part that travels.

A reading tool that walks a section of the archive and reports what it notices, with an explicit guard against drifting from noticing into telling me what to build.

A dormancy tool that surfaces what I've stopped paying attention to, written so that going quiet is never framed as a failure.

A link auditor that reads what my notes reach for and never fixes anything itself. It weighs links I made more heavily than links an AI applied on my behalf, so an assistant's habits can't masquerade as my own intent.

A documentation checker that finds where my written instructions have drifted from reality, and revises only the lines that actually drifted. Every sentence still true stays untouched, word for word.

A suggestion collector that gathers ideas flagged across months of work and ranks them by how often the material asked, rather than by how good they sound.

A batch processor with a confirmation at every stage, which flags a file that looks miscategorized instead of silently reclassifying it, and never overwrites anything.

An annotation layer that adds structure and cross-references to raw text without altering a word of it.

A conversation archiver that records both halves of a dialogue with an AI, and cross-references only my words. The record belongs to both of us. The map belongs to me.

A reading tool for outside sources that returns what's useful, what doesn't fit, and where the source and I actually disagree, on the theory that friction is more useful than agreement.

A verification tool holding the checks that catch what a browser hides: a stylesheet returning the wrong content type, an import that 404s without breaking the page, a phone-width test that was never actually testing a phone. This is the one I didn't think of.

The honest limit

Most of these were built for one user, and that user is me. I knew exactly who would read every output and what it would cost if a tool got it wrong, which is why the boundaries are drawn where they are.

That's a real limitation of a portfolio like this, so I'd rather say it than have you find it. The two most recent tools were built for other people, and they're the ones I'd point at first.

Knowing exactly who a tool is for, and what it would cost them if it went wrong, is most of the work. It's also the part that doesn't survive being handed a generic solution.
